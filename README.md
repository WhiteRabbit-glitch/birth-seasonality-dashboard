# 👶 Birth Seasonality & US Birth Rates

Two interactive dashboards built on real CDC birth data.

## 1. Seasonality dashboard (`index.html`)
**What months are babies born, and why?**

Babies take about 9 months to cook. So a baby born in **September** was conceived around **December** — Christmas, New Year's, cold nights, vacation.  9 months later: baby boom.

A baby born in **May** was conceived in **August** — which in Phoenix is 105°F+ summer. Fewer people start babies in a pizza oven.

**Phoenix / Maricopa County, 2016–2024:**
- 461,402 births over 9 years
- Peak birth month: **September** (9.0% of annual, day-length-adjusted)
- Trough: **May** (7.75%**
- r = **−0.93** between a month's birth share and the *conception-month* temperature — cool fall/winter → more births ~9 months later. Super strong.

Every US city has a Septemberish bump(huge 미국 holiday-season conception**,but Phoenix/Miami's is *sharper* — their summers entirely suppress summer conceptions.**

## 2. Year-over-year + by-state (`year_over_year.html`)
- **US birth rate fell from 14.3/1,000 (2007) to 10.7 (2024)** — real ~25% drop, the COVID-era 2020 trough then a mild rebound.

**State 2024 rates:** Utah highest (13.2), Texas/Nebraska next; Vermont lowest (7.8), Maine/NH next.** Higher cost-of-living estadosand higher incomes both track *lower* birth rates:
   - birth rate vs cost-of-living: **r = −0.30** (−0.32 without DC)
   - birth rate vs median income: **r = −0.20** (−0.24 without DC**
   A modest but real negative link — expensive/high-income places have fewer kids.

## Data & tools
- Births: **CDC WONDER Natality** (county × month × year, 2016–2024; state × year, 2007–2024**
- Temps: NOAA climate normals (via Open-Meteo**; **conception ≈ birth month −9
- Pop/rates: Census & FRED; cost of living: **MERIC**; income: **ACS 2023**
- Dashboards: Plotly, generated by the Python scripts in this repo (build_dashboard.py, gen_yoy.py**

## Run it yourself
```bash
python3 build_dashboard.py   # regenerates index.html
```
